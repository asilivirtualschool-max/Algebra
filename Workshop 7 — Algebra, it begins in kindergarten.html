Mathematics: What's the Big Idea? · Workshop 7

Algebra: it begins in kindergarten

Algebra is not a subject that starts in year 7. It is a way of thinking that either gets built across six years of primary arithmetic, or gets quietly blocked by it. This session is for both halves of a school at once.

Video: Mathematics: What's the Big Idea?, Workshop 7, guide Monica Neagoy. Smithsonian Institution Astrophysical Observatory with the Annenberg Foundation, 1997. The programme guide is at guide.pdf — a scan, so it is not searchable. Facilitation materials on this page are original.
Before the session · on your own

1. The student who was fine until algebra10 minutes

Every secondary maths department knows this student: strong on arithmetic, quick with number, and then letters arrive and they stall. Every primary teacher has taught them, and taught them well.

Name the point where algebra first goes wrong for your students.

Be specific — a topic, not a year group. "Solving equations" is too broad; "solving when the unknown appears on both sides" is a place we can look.

If you teach primary: what do you already do that you would call algebra?

Most primary teachers say "nothing". Most primary teachers do it several times a week without calling it that. Answer before you read on.

Before the session · on your own

2. Four ideas algebra needs before the letters arrive12 minutes

Each of these is learnable from age five and each is routinely left until it is too late. In every case the wrong idea students bring is not carelessness — it is a correct generalisation from the diet of examples they were fed.

One. The equals sign says "same value", not "here comes the answer"

Ask a class of ten-year-olds to fill in 8 + 4 = □ + 5. In the study that made this famous, fewer than one child in ten wrote 7. The common answers are 12 — the total of the left side — and 17, the running total of everything in sight.

The nameable wrong answer: "= means work it out and write the result."
Where it was learned: six years of number sentences with one operation on the left and one number on the right. Given that diet, "the answer goes after the equals sign" is the right generalisation. We taught it by accident, thoroughly.

The tell: a student who can recite "equals means the same as" and still computes left-to-right when you put something on both sides.

Two. A letter is not a shorthand for an object

Students meet 3a + 5a and are told, helpfully, "three apples plus five apples". It works. It keeps working for a term. It breaks the moment a is a price rather than an apple, breaks harder at a², and breaks completely when the letter has to stand for a quantity that varies rather than a specific hidden number.

There are three different jobs a letter does, and they are usually taught as if they were one: the unknown (one particular number, currently hidden, findable), the generalised number (any number, as in a + b = b + a), and the variable (a quantity that varies, in step with another). Asking which job the letter is doing today is a question most students have never been asked.

The nameable wrong answer: "n stands for a thing — a number of apples, a name, a label."
Where it was learned: from us, as a kindness. "3 apples plus 5 apples" is an accurate description of the syntax and a false account of the meaning.

The tell: ask which is bigger, n + 2 or 2n. "2n, obviously" means the letter is being read as a specific largish number rather than as something that varies. The answer is that it depends — they are equal at 2, and n + 2 is bigger below it.

Three. An expression is a finished object

Ask for the perimeter of a rectangle with sides n and 4 and many students refuse the answer 2n + 8. It doesn't look finished. It has an operation still sitting in it, and everything they have ever been asked to do with a visible operation is carry it out.

The nameable wrong answer: "2n + 8 can't be the answer — it hasn't been worked out yet." Hence the invented closure: 2n + 8 = 10n.
Where it was learned: from arithmetic, where every expression could be collapsed to a single number and always was.

The tell: students who add a spurious final step to reach a single term, or who ask "but what does it equal?" of a perfectly complete answer.

Four. A pattern has two kinds of rule, and school teaches only one

A tiled path takes 4, 7, 10, 13 tiles for lengths 1, 2, 3, 4. Almost every child finds "add 3 each time". Almost none of them, unprompted, finds the rule that takes you straight from the length to the number of tiles — and only the second kind of rule answers "how many for length 100?" without counting to a hundred.

The step-by-step rule is recursive; the direct one is a correspondence, and it is where the function concept lives. "What comes next?" is a real question that, asked exclusively, keeps students inside the recursive rule for years.

The nameable wrong answer: "The rule is +3." Followed, when pushed to length 100, by either counting or by the proportional guess — term 8 must be double term 4.
Where it was learned: a decade of pattern-continuation tasks that never once asked for the hundredth term.

The tell: term 4 of that path is 13 tiles. Ask for term 8. An immediate 26 — double term 4 — means the pattern has been read as a proportion, which it isn't: the true answer is 25, and the gap is the constant tile that doubling ignores.

Which of the four is doing the most damage in your classes, and what is your evidence?

Commit to one. If you name all four you'll have nothing to test next week.

Before the session · in your classroom

4. The six-item probe15 minutes of lesson time

Every teacher in the school gives the same six items, primary and secondary, to one class. Take the answers to the session. The point is not the score — it is that the same wrong answers appear in year 3 and year 10.

ItemAskWhat a wrong answer tells you
18 + 4 = □ + 512 or 17: the equals sign is being read as an instruction. Idea one.
2True or false, without calculating: 57 + 34 = 34 + 57Any calculating at all: they can't yet reason about structure, only about totals. Idea one.
315 + 27 = 16 + □43, or a full recalculation: the compensation is invisible to them. Relational thinking not yet available.
4Which is bigger, n + 2 or 2n?"2n" with no conditions: the letter is a specific largish number, not a variable. Idea two.
5There are five times as many students as teachers. Write that with S and T.5S = T: translated word order rather than meaning. Survives into engineering degrees. Idea two.
6A path of length 1, 2, 3, 4 needs 4, 7, 10, 13 tiles. How many for length 100? What does 3n + 1 mean here?"Add 3" and no route to 100: recursive lock. Refusing 3n + 1 as an answer: ideas three and four together.

Primary colleagues: give items 1, 2, 3 and 6 as they stand. Item 6 works from about year 3 with tiles on the desk. Secondary colleagues: give all six, and give them to your strongest class as well as your weakest — the results from the top set are the ones that change the conversation.

Your class, and the wrong answers you got — in their words where you can.

Verbatim matters. "Some struggled" tells the group nothing; "she said 12 because 8 and 4 makes 12, and then crossed it out and wrote 17" tells them everything.

Before the session · on your own

5. Is it a threshold?10 minutes

Four candidate ideas is not the same as four thresholds. Our standard is Meyer and Land's five tests, all five required, plus a sixth of our own: the wrong answer has to be nameable, and ideally traceable to the earlier rule that produced it. Here is idea one, tested in full.

TestThe equals sign as a statement of equal value
TransformativePasses. Every equation the student meets afterwards reads differently: not a calculation to perform but a balance to preserve.
IrreversiblePasses. You cannot go back to seeing 8 + 4 = □ + 5 as "twelve". Teachers routinely can't reconstruct why their students find it hard.
IntegrativePasses. It ties together the commutative and associative properties, equation solving, equivalent expressions, and later identities and function notation — which stay four unrelated topics without it.
TroublesomePasses. Not merely hard: counter-intuitive against six years of successful practice, and it produces inert knowledge — the definition can be recited while the behaviour stays operational.
DiscursivePasses. Talk changes. "Is the same value as", "both sides", and justification from structure — "it must be, because both are 34 and 57 added" — instead of justification from calculation.
Nameable misconceptionPasses. "= means write the answer next", traceable to a diet of single-operation number sentences.

The threshold statement. Under thirty words, with the contrast made explicit:

"The equals sign states that two expressions have the same value. It is not an instruction to calculate and write the result."

Now do one of the other three. Which tests does it pass, and where does it fail?

Failing a test is a useful result, not a wasted exercise. An idea that is merely difficult is not a threshold, and calling it one devalues the ones that are. Write the threshold statement too, under thirty words, with the "not X" in it.

Group session · facilitator

6. Session agenda75 minutes

Seat primary and secondary teachers in mixed pairs before anyone sits down with their own department. The entire value of this session is in that pairing. If the two phases sit separately you will get two conversations about the other phase's failings and no shared work.

TimeWhat happens
0–5Frame it. Algebra is not a topic that arrives in year 7. Every year group in this room is already teaching it or already blocking it.
5–20The probe. In mixed pairs, swap the wrong answers you collected. Read them out by item, not by year group, so the room hears item 1 from year 3 and year 10 back to back. No analysis yet.
20–32Diagnose. Sort the wrong answers into the four ideas. Expect disagreement about items 5 and 6 — the argument is the learning.
32–50The vertical audit. The core exercise. Run it as below.
50–62The design problem. Run it as below.
62–70What this costs. Discuss honestly. Do not skip this.
70–75Commitment. One change, one class, a date.

The vertical audit 32–50

Two columns on the wall. Which of our own routines plant these four ideas, and which block them?

Candidates that usually surface as blockers, and are worth having ready if the room is slow to start:

  • Worksheets where the answer box is always on the right of the equals sign.
  • "Sum" used to mean "the question", so = becomes the boundary between question and answer.
  • Pattern work that only ever asks what comes next.
  • "Three apples plus five apples" as the explanation for collecting like terms.
  • Marking a correct 2n + 8 as incomplete.
  • Mental strategies taught as tricks — "just add 1 to one and take 1 off the other" — without ever asking why it works, which is the generalisation.

And the routines that already do the work, which primary colleagues usually have not been given credit for: missing-number problems in any position; true-or-false number sentences; "make ten" and compensation strategies when the reasoning is made explicit; asking whether something is always true; growing patterns built with physical materials where the structure stays visible.

Facilitator note. Insist on named routines and named year groups, not principles. "We should use more relational thinking" changes nothing on Monday. "Year 4's mental maths starter will ask why the compensation works, every Tuesday, from next week" does.

The design problem 50–62

Design a task where the wrong idea visibly fails — without telling anyone they are wrong. Take one of the four. The task has to be one where a student holding the misconception gets a result that does not work, and notices.

The constraint is the exercise. Telling is what we already do, and telling is what produced students who can recite "equals means the same as" while computing left to right.

Facilitator notes. Push for the actual numbers, the actual prompt, the actual sequence. Two things reliably surface, both worth naming out loud:

  • The student has to commit to a prediction before the task runs, or they will not notice that their idea failed. They will absorb the result as a special case.
  • One counter-example is rarely enough. Where the wrong idea has years of successful use behind it, a single failure gets filed as a curiosity that leaves the general rule intact.

What this costs 62–70

Relational thinking is slower than procedural fluency to teach and does not show up on the next assessment. Primary colleagues are being asked to spend time on structure that their own results do not reward, for the benefit of a department they may never meet.

Ask the room directly: what would we stop doing, and who has the authority to agree it?

This question decides whether the session changes anything. If nobody in the room controls a scheme of work, the audit on the wall is decoration. Get the primary maths lead and the secondary head of department in the same room or postpone the session.

After the session

7. Your commitment

One routine you will change, with a class and a date.

A routine, not a lesson. The four ideas are built by what happens every week, not by one good lesson on equality.

What will you give up to make room for it?

If the answer is nothing, the change probably won't happen. Better to know now.

Re-run one probe item with the same class in six weeks. What changed?

Item 1 or item 2 is the cheapest test — two minutes of a lesson. Without this, nobody in the school will know whether any of it worked.
